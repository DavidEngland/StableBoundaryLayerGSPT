When re-examining $K_m$, $K_h$, and $\text{Ri}_g$ strictly as **diagnostic metrics** (independent of prognostic time-stepping), the objective is to allow **external numerical models** (e.g., WRF SCM, LES benchmark datasets, or observational tower profiles from CASES-99 / FLOSS) to evaluate the GSPT manifold closure without running or modifying the prognostic solver.

Here is a comprehensive breakdown of the diagnostic formulations, grid-discretization requirements, and best practices for inter-model evaluation.

---

### 1. Mathematical Diagnostic Definitions

#### A. Gradient Richardson Number ($\text{Ri}_g$)

The gradient Richardson number measures the local ratio of buoyant suppression to shear production:

$$\text{Ri}_g(z_{i+1/2}) = \frac{N^2}{S^2} = \frac{\frac{g}{\theta_a} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}}{\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right)^2}$$

* **Diagnostic Discretization** (Interface level $z_{i+1/2}$):

$$\left.\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}\right\vert{}_{i+1/2} = \frac{\phi_{i+1} - \phi_i}{z_{i+1} - z_i}$$


* **Numerical Safeguards for Diagnostics**:

$$\text{Ri}_g(z_{i+1/2}) = \frac{\frac{g}{\theta_a} \left(\frac{\theta_{i+1} - \theta_i}{\Delta z_i}\right)}{\max\left[\left(\frac{U_{i+1} - U_i}{\Delta z_i}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{V_{i+1} - V_i}{\Delta z_i}\right)^2, \; \epsilon_{\text{Ri}}\right]}$$


* Set $\epsilon_{\text{Ri}} \approx 10^{-12} \text{ s}^{-2}$ to prevent division by zero in still or geostrophic equilibrium layers.
* For plotting across numerical models, display $\text{Ri}_g$ using an inverse hyperbolic sine scale (`xscale=:asinh`) to smoothly capture both near-critical transition ($\text{Ri}_g \approx 0.25$) and strongly stable extremes ($\text{Ri}_g > 10$).



---

#### B. Momentum Eddy Diffusivity ($K_m$) Diagnostic

To evaluate $K_m$ diagnostically from any arbitrary profile state $(U, V, \theta)$ on interface height $z_{i+1/2}$:

1. **Local Shears & Stability Response**:

$$S^2 = \left(\frac{\Delta U}{\Delta z}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta z}\right)^2, \quad \text{Arg} = \text{clamp}\left(\beta_{\text{stab}} \frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta z} \frac{\ell_{\text{eff}}}{\theta_a}, -40, 40\right)$$


$$G = g_{\text{max}} \tanh(\text{Arg})$$


2. **Net Forcing & Option-2 Fold Manifold**:

$$\Delta_{\text{local}} = \eta S^2 - K_{\text{buoy}} G$$


$$D_{\text{local}} = \beta_{\text{gspt}}^2 + 4 \Delta_{\text{local}}$$


$$\sqrt{D}_{\text{reg}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\text{local}} + \sqrt{D_{\text{local}}^2 + \xi^2}\right)}$$


$$q^* = \frac{1}{2} \ell_0 \left(\beta_{\text{gspt}} + \sqrt{D}_{\text{reg}}\right)$$


3. **Activation Gate & $K_m$ Output**:

$$\tilde{e}^* = (q^*)^2, \quad \psi_{\text{gate}} = \frac{\sqrt{\tilde{e}^*}}{\sqrt{\tilde{e}^*} + \alpha_{\text{gate}}}$$


$$K_m(z_{i+1/2}) = K_{\text{min, interior}} + \ell_{\text{eff}} \sqrt{\psi_{\text{gate}} \, \tilde{e}^* + \delta}$$



---

#### C. Heat Diffusivity ($K_h$) and Turbulent Prandtl Number ($\text{Pr}_t$)

$$K_h(z_{i+1/2}) = \frac{K_m(z_{i+1/2})}{\max\left[\text{Pr}_t(z_{i+1/2}), \, \epsilon_{\text{Pr}}\right]}$$

Where $\text{Pr}_t$ is evaluated either statically ($\text{Pr}_{t,\text{base}}$) or dynamically:


$$\text{Pr}_t(z_{i+1/2}) = \text{Pr}_{t,\text{base}} + \text{Pr}_{t,\text{slope}} \tanh(\max(0, G))$$

* **Diagnostic Output**: Outputting both raw $K_{h,\text{raw}}$ and floor-regularized $K_h = \sqrt{K_{h,\text{raw}}^2 + K_{\text{exchange,min}}^2}$ allows external models to isolate pure turbulence closure decay from baseline numerical background floors.

---

### 2. Best Practices for Inter-Model Diagnostic Comparison

When comparing SCM diagnostic outputs against external numerical models (e.g., LES benchmarks or 3D mesoscale models):

1. **Non-Uniform Grid Support**:
Avoid assuming uniform cell spacing $\Delta z$. Evaluate vertical gradients using local interface grid intervals:

$$\Delta z_i = z_{\text{center}, i+1} - z_{\text{center}, i}$$


2. **Local Flux vs. Bulk Surface Exchange**:
* **Cell Interfaces ($z > z_1$)**: Evaluated via gradient diffusion $K_m \frac{\partial U}{\partial z}$ and $K_h \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}$.
* **Skin/Surface ($z_0 \le z \le z_1$)**: External models usually output surface sensible heat flux $H = \rho c_p C_H \vert{}V_1\vert{} (T_s - \theta_1)$ using Monin–Obukhov bulk resistance $C_H$. Comparing surface $K_h$ directly to $C_H$ requires converting bulk drag to an equivalent bulk velocity scale $u_* C_H / C_D^{1/2}$.


3. **Decoupling Diagnostic Calculations from ODE Solvers**:
Ensure diagnostic routines take pure input arrays `(zc, zf, U, V, theta, T_s, p)` without assuming active time-integration buffers.

### 3. Standalone Julia Blueprint for Model Evaluation

Below is a lightweight, zero-side-effect diagnostic function that can be used by external scripts or other numerical models to evaluate $K_m$, $K_h$, $\text{Pr}_t$, and $\text{Ri}_g$ from arbitrary 1D profile states:

```julia
"""
    diagnose_profiles(zc, zf, U, V, theta, T_s, p)

Diagnostic-only evaluator for external model states or observational profiles.
Returns a NamedTuple containing face-centered Ri_g, Km, Kh, Pr_t, and Delta.
"""
function diagnose_profiles(zc::Vector{Float64}, zf::Vector{Float64},
                           U::Vector{Float64}, V::Vector{Float64},
                           theta::Vector{Float64}, T_s::Float64, p)
    N = length(zc)
    N_faces = N - 1

    Ri_g = zeros(Float64, N_faces)
    Km   = zeros(Float64, N_faces)
    Kh   = zeros(Float64, N_faces)
    Pr_t = zeros(Float64, N_faces)
    Delta= zeros(Float64, N_faces)

    g = 9.81
    theta_a = p.theta_a
    l0 = p.l_0
    kappa = 0.4
    beta_gspt = hasproperty(p, :beta_gspt) ? p.beta_gspt : p.beta
    beta_stab = hasproperty(p, :beta_stab) ? p.beta_stab : 5.0
    alpha_gate = hasproperty(p, :alpha_gate) ? p.alpha_gate : 1.0e-3
    eta = p.eta
    K_buoy = p.K_buoy
    delta = p.delta
    xi = p.xi

    for i in 1:N_faces
        dz = zc[i+1] - zc[i]
        dU_dz = (U[i+1] - U[i]) / dz
        dV_dz = (V[i+1] - V[i]) / dz
        dth_dz = (theta[i+1] - theta[i]) / dz

        # Face height and neutral mixing length
        z_face = zf[i+1]
        ell_neutral = (kappa * z_face) / (1.0 + (kappa * z_face) / l0)

        # Local shear and Richardson number
        S2 = dU_dz^2 + dV_dz^2
        N2 = (g / theta_a) * dth_dz
        Ri_g[i] = N2 / max(S2, 1.0e-12)

        # GSPT Manifold closure
        arg = clamp(beta_stab * dth_dz * ell_neutral / theta_a, -40.0, 40.0)
        G = tanh(arg)

        Delta_loc = eta * S2 - K_buoy * G
        D_loc = beta_gspt^2 + 4.0 * Delta_loc
        sqrt_D_reg = sqrt(0.5 * (D_loc + sqrt(D_loc^2 + xi^2)))
        q_star = 0.5 * l0 * (beta_gspt + sqrt_D_reg)

        tilde_e = q_star^2
        psi_gate = sqrt(tilde_e) / (sqrt(tilde_e) + alpha_gate)

        pr_t_loc = p.pr_t_base + (p.use_dynamic_pr_t ? p.pr_t_slope * tanh(max(0.0, G)) : 0.0)
        km_loc = p.k_min_interior + ell_neutral * sqrt(psi_gate * tilde_e + delta)
        kh_loc = km_loc / max(pr_t_loc, eps(Float64))

        Ri_g[i] = Ri_g[i]
        Km[i] = km_loc
        Kh[i] = kh_loc
        Pr_t[i] = pr_t_loc
        Delta[i] = Delta_loc
    end

    return (z_faces=zf[2:end-1], Ri_g=Ri_g, Km=Km, Kh=Kh, Pr_t=Pr_t, Delta=Delta)
end

```

The reason Figure 7 ($K_m$ and $K_h$ plotted against $\text{Ri}_g$) looks like a **multi-valued scatter cloud with distinct branches**, rather than a single neat curve, lies in a fundamental physical difference between **traditional single-valued closures** and **Gradient System Fold Theory (GSPT)**.

---

### 1. Traditional Schemes vs. GSPT: The Core Difference

#### Traditional $\text{Ri}_g$-based Schemes (Louis 1979, Mellor–Yamada, Monin–Obukhov)

In most operational numerical models (WRF, ECMWF, GFS), eddy diffusivities are parameterized as **1D deterministic functions** of the local Gradient Richardson number $\text{Ri}_g$:


$$K_{m,h} = \ell^2 S \, f_{m,h}(\text{Ri}_g)$$


where $\text{Ri}_g$ is the dimensionless ratio of thermal buoyancy to mechanical shear:


$$\text{Ri}_g = \frac{N^2}{S^2} = \frac{\frac{g}{\theta_0} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}}{\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\right)^2}$$


Because these models force $f(\text{Ri}_g)$ to be a single-valued function, plotting $K$ versus $\text{Ri}_g$ in a traditional model yields a **single, monotonic line** that drops sharply to zero (or a background floor) once $\text{Ri}_g \ge \text{Ri}_{\text{crit}} \approx 0.25$.

---

#### The GSPT Closure

In GSPT, eddy diffusivities are **not** single-valued functions of the dimensionless ratio $\text{Ri}_g$. Instead, $K_m$ and $K_h$ are governed by an internal state energy variable $q^*$ that depends on the **dimensional fold parameter** $\Delta$:

$$\Delta = \eta S^2 - K_{\text{buoy}} G\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}\right)$$

$$q^* = \frac{1}{2} \ell_0 \left( \beta + \sqrt{D_{\text{reg}}} \right), \quad D = \beta^2 + 4\Delta$$

$$K_m = K_{\text{min}} + \ell_{\text{eff}} \sqrt{\psi_{\text{gate}} (q^*)^2 + \delta}$$

$$\text{Ri}_g = \frac{N^2}{S^2} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \Delta = \eta S^2 - K_{\text{buoy}} G\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}\right)$$

---

Figure 7 exhibits four major physical features:

#### Feature A: Multi-Valued Vertical Scatter (Non-Uniqueness)

* **What you see:** For a single value of $\text{Ri}_g$ (e.g., $\text{Ri}_g = 0.3$), $K_m$ ranges across **1 to 2 orders of magnitude** (e.g., from $0.05 \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ to $0.8 \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$).
* **Why it happens:** $\text{Ri}_g = N^2/S^2$ is a ratio.
* A high-shear / high-stratification regime ($S^2 = 0.1 \text{ s}^{-2}, N^2 = 0.03 \text{ s}^{-2}$) yields $\text{Ri}_g = 0.3$. Because absolute shear $S^2$ is high, $\Delta > 0$, driving the system onto the **high-$K_m$ turbulent state**.
* A weak-shear / weak-stratification regime ($S^2 = 0.001 \text{ s}^{-2}, N^2 = 0.0003 \text{ s}^{-2}$) yields the *exact same* $\text{Ri}_g = 0.3$. But because absolute shear is low, $\Delta < 0$, collapsing $K_m$ down to the **laminar background floor**.



#### Feature B: Persistent Mixing Above Critical Richardson Number ($\text{Ri}_g > 0.25$)

* **What you see:** Significant turbulent diffusivities ($K_m \sim 0.1 \text{--} 1.0 \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$) exist far above $\text{Ri}_g = 0.25$, extending to $\text{Ri}_g \sim 1.0 \text{--} 5.0$.
* **Why it happens:** Linear stability theory (Miles–Howard) predicts flow becomes laminar when $\text{Ri}_g > 0.25$. However, observational field campaigns (**CASES-99, FLOSS, SHEBA**) consistently measure active turbulence at $\text{Ri}_g \gg 0.25$. GSPT naturally captures this **subcritical finite-amplitude turbulence** via the upper branch of the fold bifurcation without relying on artificial "long-tail" tuning functions.

#### Feature C: Two-Branch Structure (Active vs. Decoupled Regimes)

* **What you see:** Data points cluster into an **upper band** (active turbulent boundary layer) and a **lower band** (decoupled surface layer near $K_{\text{min}}$).

### The Physical Reason $\text{Ri}_g$ Fails as Atmosphere Becomes Very Stable

Boundary layer meteorologists and turbulence modelers widely recognize that local Richardson-number closures ($K \propto f(\text{Ri}_g)$) work well in **unstable and near-neutral regimes**, but fail systematically in the **strongly stable boundary layer (SBL)**.

Here are the four primary physical mechanisms that explain why this happens:

#### 1. Breakdown of Local Monin–Obukhov Similarity (MOST)

* **Unstable / Near-Neutral:** Thermal plumes and shear eddies are large, coherent, and span the depth of the surface layer. Local vertical gradients ($\partial U/\partial z$, $\partial \theta/\partial z$) are directly coupled to local turbulent fluxes ($w'u'$, $w'\theta'$).
* **Strongly Stable:** Stable stratification suppresses vertical parcel displacement, shrinking eddy sizes down to scales smaller than the model grid spacing. Turbulence becomes **patchy, localized, and decoupled** from the surface. A single instantaneous local gradient $\text{Ri}_g$ no longer uniquely determines the net vertical flux.

#### 2. Hysteresis and Memory Effects (Non-equilibrium Turbulence)

* **Unstable / Near-Neutral:** Production and dissipation of turbulence kinetic energy (TKE) are nearly in local equilibrium ($\mathcal{P} \approx \varepsilon$). $K$ adjusts almost instantly to changes in shear.
* **Strongly Stable:** SBL turbulence exhibits strong **state hysteresis**. The flow behaves differently depending on whether turbulence is *collapsing* (due to runaway radiational cooling) or *rebuilding* (due to shear buildup from a Low-Level Jet). A single diagnostic curve $K(\text{Ri}_g)$ cannot capture both paths—it lacks a state-memory variable.

#### 3. Intermittency and Internal Gravity Waves

* In strongly stable conditions ($\text{Ri}_g > 0.25$), continuous shear-driven turbulence collapses.
* However, vertical transport does not drop to zero; it occurs in **intermittent bursts** triggered by Kelvin–Helmholtz (K-H) instabilities, density currents, or internal gravity waves (IGWs).
* Waves transport momentum without transporting sensible heat in the same way, causing the turbulent Prandtl number ($\text{Pr}_t = K_m/K_h$) to diverge—a behavior a simple 1D $\text{Ri}_g$ function cannot reproduce.

#### 4. Decoupling and Radiative Competition

* When wind speeds fall below a critical threshold, turbulent heat transport can no longer compete with surface radiative cooling.
* The surface **decouples** from the air above, creating an extreme surface inversion capped by a Low-Level Jet (LLJ). Under these conditions, the local $\text{Ri}_g$ can spike to huge values ($\text{Ri}_g \gg 1$) while elevated shear above the inversion continues to drive active mixing.

### 4.X.1 Non-Uniqueness in Richardson Number Space

For instance, a high-shear layer in the lower boundary layer ($S = 0.08\text{ s}^{-1}, N^2 = 0.0032\text{ s}^{-2}$) and a weak-shear region near the boundary layer top ($S = 0.008\text{ s}^{-1}, N^2 = 0.000032\text{ s}^{-2}$) both yield $\text{Ri}_g = 0.5$. * **Height Stratification (Figure 7b):** The upper branch corresponds predominantly to the active near-surface layer and the shear zone beneath the low-level jet (LLJ), whereas the lower branch reflects the decoupled atmosphere above the inversion height.

### 4.X.2 Manifold Collapse onto the Fold Invariant $\Delta$

To resolve the non-uniqueness inherent in $\text{Ri}_g$ parameterizations, Gradient System Fold Theory (GSPT) governs turbulent transport through the dimensional fold invariant $\Delta$:

$$\Delta = \eta S^2 - K_{\text{buoy}} G\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}\right)$$

where $\eta$ sets the shear-production time scale, $K_{\text{buoy}}$ scales buoyancy destruction, and $G(\cdot)$ is a bounded saturation function representing thermal stability. The state kinetic energy parameter $q^*(\Delta)$ is obtained directly from the root of the folded manifold:

$$q^*(\Delta) = \frac{1}{2} \ell_0 \left( \beta_{\text{gspt}} + \sqrt{\beta_{\text{gspt}}^2 + 4\Delta} \right)$$

Plotting momentum diffusivity $K_m$ directly against $\Delta$ yields the primary theoretical result of this framework (Figure 7c). The multi-valued scatter cloud observed in $\text{Ri}_g$ space collapses into a tight, single-valued, monotonic curve governed by $q^*(\Delta)$.

This collapse demonstrates that $\Delta$—which balances the *absolute* rates of mechanical shear production and buoyant destruction—is the natural state coordinate of the turbulent boundary layer. The threshold $\Delta = 0$ marks the critical fold point:

1. **Super-critical Fold Region ($\Delta > 0$):** Shear production dominates buoyancy destruction, locking the local fluid parcel onto the active turbulent branch.
2. **Sub-critical Fold Region ($\Delta < 0$):** Buoyancy destruction exceeds shear production, forcing $q^*$ toward zero and driving the local diffusivity down to the regularization threshold $K_{\text{min}}$.

By operating on $\Delta$ rather than $\text{Ri}_g$, the GSPT closure naturally accommodates subcritical turbulence at $\text{Ri}_g \gg 0.25$ without requiring artificial "long-tail" empirical adjustment functions (e.g., operational tail-tuning in WRF or ECMWF schemes).

### 4.X.3 Dynamic Prandtl Number Differentiation

The dynamic relationship between momentum diffusivity $K_m$ and heat diffusivity $K_h$ is illustrated through the effective turbulent Prandtl number $\text{Pr}_t = K_m / K_h$ in Figure 7d.

In near-neutral conditions ($\text{Ri}_g \to 0$), the ratio approaches $\text{Pr}_t \approx 1.0$, indicating isotropic turbulent transport of scalar heat and momentum. However, as stability increases ($\text{Ri}_g > 0.1$), $\text{Pr}_t$ increases monotonically, reaching values between $2.0$ and $5.0$.

Physically, strong thermal stratification acts directly against vertical buoyancy fluctuations ($w'\theta'$), suppressing heat transport. Conversely, pressure-strain correlations sustain horizontal-to-vertical momentum transfers ($w'u'$), enabling shear-driven momentum diffusion to persist even when thermal transport is strongly suppressed. The GSPT framework captures this differential transport behavior naturally through its coupled $K_m$ and $K_h$ formulation, providing a realistic representation of nocturnal surface cooling and inversion strengthening.

\section{The GSPT Closure Manifold and Dimensional Reduction}
\label{sec:gspt_closure_manifold}

A central challenge in modeling the strongly stable boundary layer (SBL) is the observed failure of local flux-gradient closures based solely on the gradient Richardson number,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Ri}_g = \frac{N^2}{S^2} = \frac{\frac{g}{\theta_0}\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}}{\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right)^2}.
\label{eq:rig_definition}
\end{equation}
In traditional 1D atmospheric closures \citep[e.g.,][]{Louis1979, MellorYamada1982}, eddy diffusivities for momentum ($K_m$) and heat ($K_h$) are parameterized as single-valued functions $K = \ell^2 S f(\mathrm{Ri}_g)$. While this formulation performs well in unstable and near-neutral regimes where large coherent structures couple local gradients directly to fluxes, it collapses under strongly stable conditions. Observational field campaigns \citep[e.g., CASES-99, FLOSS;][]{Mahrt1999} consistently show that active turbulence persists at super-critical Richardson numbers ($\mathrm{Ri}_g \gg 0.25$), generating significant scatter that cannot be represented by a single diagnostic curve.

\subsection{Non-Uniqueness in Richardson Number Space}
\label{subsec:rig_non_uniqueness}

When simulated eddy diffusivities from the single-column model (SCM) are projected onto the classical $\mathrm{Ri}_g$ axis, they form a broad, multi-valued distribution spanning up to two orders of magnitude at a fixed Richardson number (Figures~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}a,b). The dominant structure of this scatter arises from dimensional reduction: projection of the higher-dimensional closure manifold onto the one-dimensional Richardson-number coordinate necessarily produces a many-to-one mapping. Numerical variability may broaden the distribution but does not generate its fundamental topology.

Because $\mathrm{Ri}_g$ is a dimensionless ratio of buoyancy frequency squared ($N^2$) to shear squared ($S^2$), vastly different physical states can yield identical values of $\mathrm{Ri}_g$. For instance, a high-shear layer in the lower boundary layer ($S = 0.08\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $N^2 = 0.0032\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}$) and a weak-shear region near the boundary layer top ($S = 0.008\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $N^2 = 0.000032\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}$) both yield $\mathrm{Ri}_g = 0.5$. However, the high-shear state possesses sufficient kinetic energy to sustain active turbulence, whereas the weak-shear state drops toward the laminar background floor ($K_{\mathrm{min}}$).

Color-coding the scatter by absolute shear magnitude $S$ (Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}a) and vertical height $z$ (Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}b) exposes this structural organization:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shear Ordering (Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}a):} For any super-critical Richardson number ($\mathrm{Ri}_g > 0.25$), data points with high shear ($S > 0.04\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) cluster along the upper, highly diffusive branch ($K_m \sim 10^{-1}\text{--}10^{0}\,\mathrm{m}^2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$), while low-shear points drop to the lower branch.
    \item \textbf{Height Stratification (Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}b):} The upper branch corresponds predominantly to the active near-surface layer and the shear zone beneath the low-level jet (LLJ), whereas the lower branch reflects the decoupled atmosphere above the inversion height.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Manifold Collapse onto the Fold Invariant $\Delta$}
\label{subsec:manifold_collapse}

To resolve the non-uniqueness inherent in $\mathrm{Ri}_g$ parameterizations, Gradient System Fold Theory (GSPT) governs turbulent transport through the dimensional fold invariant $\Delta$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \eta S^2 - K_{\mathrm{buoy}} G\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}\right),
\label{eq:delta_definition}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ sets the shear-production time scale, $K_{\mathrm{buoy}}$ scales buoyancy destruction, and $G(\cdot)$ is a bounded saturation function representing thermal stability. Unlike the gradient Richardson number, which is a nondimensional ratio, $\Delta$ retains the absolute competition between mechanical production and buoyancy destruction. Consequently, states with identical $\mathrm{Ri}_g$ but different shear intensity occupy different values of $\Delta$, eliminating the degeneracy inherent in $\mathrm{Ri}_g$-based closures.

The state kinetic energy parameter $q^*(\Delta)$ is obtained from the equilibrium solution of the regularized fast subsystem:
\begin{equation}
q^*(\Delta) = \frac{1}{2} \ell_0 \left( \beta_{\mathrm{gspt}} + \sqrt{\beta_{\mathrm{gspt}}^2 + 4\Delta} \right).
\label{eq:qstar_definition}
\end{equation}

Plotting momentum diffusivity $K_m$ directly against $\Delta$ reveals the principal geometric consequence of the GSPT closure (Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}c). The multi-valued scatter cloud observed in $\mathrm{Ri}_g$ space collapses onto the one-dimensional closure manifold parameterized by $\Delta$.

This collapse demonstrates that $\Delta$---which balances the \emph{absolute} rates of mechanical shear production and buoyant destruction---is the natural state coordinate of the turbulent boundary layer. The threshold $\Delta = 0$ marks the critical fold point:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Super-critical Fold Region ($\Delta > 0$):} Shear production dominates buoyancy destruction, locking the local fluid parcel onto the active turbulent branch.
    \item \textbf{Sub-critical Fold Region ($\Delta < 0$):} Buoyancy destruction exceeds shear production, forcing $q^*$ toward zero and driving the local diffusivity down to the regularization threshold $K_{\mathrm{min}}$.
\end{enumerate}

Both $K_m$ and $K_h$ are projections of the same underlying equilibrium manifold $q^*(\Delta)$. Their apparent scatter in Richardson-number space therefore reflects information lost during projection rather than independent variability in the diffusivities themselves. By operating on $\Delta$ rather than $\mathrm{Ri}_g$, the GSPT closure naturally accommodates subcritical turbulence at $\mathrm{Ri}_g \gg 0.25$ without requiring artificial ``long-tail'' empirical adjustment functions.

\subsection{Dynamic Prandtl Number Differentiation}
\label{subsec:prandtl_number}

The dynamic relationship between momentum diffusivity $K_m$ and heat diffusivity $K_h$ is illustrated through the effective turbulent Prandtl number $\mathrm{Pr}_t = K_m / K_h$ in Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold}d.

In near-neutral conditions ($\mathrm{Ri}_g \to 0$), the ratio approaches $\mathrm{Pr}_t \approx 1.0$, indicating isotropic turbulent transport of scalar heat and momentum. However, as stability increases ($\mathrm{Ri}_g > 0.1$), $\mathrm{Pr}_t$ increases monotonically, reaching values between $2.0$ and $5.0$.

Physically, strong thermal stratification acts directly against vertical buoyancy fluctuations ($w'\theta'$), suppressing heat transport. Conversely, pressure-strain correlations sustain horizontal-to-vertical momentum transfers ($w'u'$), enabling shear-driven momentum diffusion to persist even when thermal transport is strongly suppressed. The increase of $\mathrm{Pr}_t$ with stability is therefore an emergent property of the coupled fast--slow dynamics rather than an independently prescribed empirical function, providing a realistic representation of nocturnal surface cooling and inversion strengthening.

Collectively, Figure~\ref{fig:closure_manifold} demonstrates that the apparent failure of Richardson-number closures is not evidence of intrinsically stochastic turbulence. Rather, it reflects the projection of a higher-dimensional deterministic closure manifold onto a one-dimensional stability coordinate. The GSPT framework restores this missing geometric structure by parameterizing turbulent transport through the fold invariant $\Delta$, yielding a physically interpretable, mathematically single-valued closure while remaining fully compatible with conventional eddy-diffusivity formulations.
